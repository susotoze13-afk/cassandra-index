 Update 366 – IAEA Director General Statement on Situation in Ukraine 
 11 September 2026 
 Vienna, Austria 
 67/2026 
 This week’s restoration of off-site electricity to the Zaporizhzhya Nuclear Power Plant (ZNPP) has eased an immediate risk of station blackout, but further action is needed to stabilize the plant’s increasingly fragile power situation and replenish its depleted diesel fuel reserves, IAEA Director General Rafael Mariano Grossi said today. 
 The ZNPP had lost connection to its sole remaining external power line – the 330 kilovolt (kV) Ferosplavna-1 – on 20 August following military activity on the northern side of the Dnipro River. For nearly three weeks, the plant was therefore forced to rely on emergency diesel generators to power its essential safety and security functions, most importantly the pumps that cool all six reactors and their spent fuel. 
 To address this serious nuclear safety concern, the IAEA negotiated a temporary localized ceasefire – the seventh such arrangement for urgent nuclear safety-related work – enabling demining and repair activities to be conducted from 5 to 7 September. An IAEA field team observed the work, and the line was reconnected on the evening of 7 September, ending the ZNPP’s 26th loss of off-site power during the conflict. 
 “Both sides have engaged constructively with the IAEA to help make this localized ceasefire possible, showing that practical steps can be taken, even in extremely difficult circumstances, to improve nuclear safety,” Director General Grossi told the IAEA Board of Governors earlier this week, shortly before the line was reconnected. 
 The Director General stressed that the ZNPP remains vulnerable and said further measures are needed to strengthen nuclear safety and security, including the transport of additional diesel fuel to the site. 
 Even with external electricity restored, sufficient diesel fuel remains vital, especially as the ZNPP has experienced 10 losses of off-site power since June. The latest loss of off-site power significantly depleted the ZNPP’s diesel reserves, increasing the risk of a station blackout if they are not replenished before any further cut in external electricity. 
 However, re-supply remains difficult because of the military situation in the area, and the off-site fuel storage is reportedly empty. The approaching cold season could add to these pressures, as diesel may also be needed for mobile boilers used for district heating and for the site’s diesel steam generators, which support wastewater treatment. 
 Frequent interruptions to the plant’s power lines and its reliance on emergency diesel generators pose serious risks and violate the IAEA’s Seven Indispensable Pillars for ensuring nuclear safety and security during an armed conflict and the Five Concrete Principles for protecting the ZNPP, Director General Grossi said. 
 “A reliable supply of electricity is essential to powering the ZNPP’s safety and security functions, most notably the pumps that cool all six reactors and their spent fuel pools,” he added. 
 Before the line was repaired, the plant had reduced the number of operating diesel generators from four to two to conserve fuel. It had also begun operating cooling pumps intermittently, while still maintaining the temperature of the reactor primary circuits and spent fuel pools. 
 The ZNPP’s other external power line – the 750 kV Dniprovska line – remains unavailable despite repairs completed in June under a previous IAEA-brokered ceasefire because of severe damage at the associated electrical substation. Before the conflict, Europe’s largest nuclear power plant (NPP) had 10 power lines available. 
 The security situation around the plant also remains a serious concern, Director General Grossi said. The IAEA team at the ZNPP continues to report frequent military activity in the vicinity of the site and the nearby city of Enerhodar, where plant staff live. 
 This week, the team was informed of attacks in Enerhodar on 6 September that resulted in nine injuries and one fatality. It was also informed of attacks on 9 September on a civilian vehicle in front of the site’s training centre, which is outside of the site’s protected area, and on the training centre itself. The reported timing of the attacks corresponded with explosions and drones heard by the IAEA team at the site. The team also heard several rounds of sustained gunfire at near distances on 10 September and were informed that drones were detected near the site’s perimeter. 
 Military activity also continued to be reported near other nuclear sites in Ukraine. This week, the South Ukraine NPP detected 13 drones in its monitoring area, while the Chornobyl site detected 15 drones. The Rivne and Khmelnytskyy NPPs each reported detecting three and two drones respectively. The IAEA team at the Khmelnytskyy NPP also had to shelter on 9 September because of an air raid alert and were requested to go to shelter again on 10 September. 
 The IAEA’s assistance programme continues to provide indispensable support where it is needed most. More than 250 deliveries of equipment have now been provided to Ukraine, with a total value exceeding €27 million. 
 “They include nuclear safety and security equipment, power supply systems, radiation monitoring instruments, physical protection equipment and medical assistance. Additional support has continued through our dedicated programmes addressing the consequences of the Kakhovka dam destruction and the safety and security of radioactive sources,” Director General Grossi said. 
 In the latest delivery, first aid and emergency kits were provided to Ukraine’s State Nuclear Regulatory Inspectorate (SNRIU), made possible with funds from the United States. 
 “Sustaining these efforts will require continued support from Member States. Current unfunded priority needs amount to approximately €17 million. I encourage those in a position to do so to continue supporting the Agency’s efforts in preventing a nuclear accident from adding to the suffering during this conflict,” he told the Board of Governors.
